Designing a Multimodal Perception Encoder

The question you already know is coming: "design a multimodal perception encoder for an AV." This page builds the vocabulary and mental models to answer it from understanding, not from a memorized script.

1. The sensor suite and why each one exists

Camera, LiDAR, radar are not redundant — they fail differently

Every sensor on an AV is there because it covers a hole the others leave open. The interview trap is treating sensors as interchangeable "more data." The real design question is: which failure mode does each sensor patch?

  • Camera — dense semantic texture (lane markings, traffic light color, pedestrian pose, text on signs). No native metric depth: a single camera gives you a ray, not a point. Fails in low light, glare, rain-on-lens, and is scale-ambiguous without stereo or learned depth.
  • LiDAR — sparse but metrically exact 3D points via time-of-flight. Illumination-invariant (works at night), but degrades in fog/rain/snow (returns scatter off particulates) and is expensive, mechanically fragile (spinning units), and gives you almost no semantic texture — a point cloud can't tell you a light is red.
  • Radar — coarse spatial resolution but direct Doppler velocity per return, and it shrugs off fog/rain/snow better than any other modality because millimeter waves pass through small particulates. Historically low angular resolution, though 4D imaging radar is closing that gap.
  • IMU / wheel odometry / ego-motion — not a "perception" sensor in the object-detection sense, but it supplies the vehicle's own 6-DoF motion at high frequency, which every other stream needs for temporal alignment and motion compensation.
How to think about it: don't ask "which sensor is best," ask "what is the joint failure surface?" Camera and radar both struggle with texture-less scenes in different ways; camera and LiDAR both struggle in heavy precipitation but for different physical reasons (photon starvation vs. beam scattering). Fusion exists specifically to make single-point-of-failure conditions rare, not to average sensors together.

Terms — Sensor suite

  • Time-of-flight (ToF) — LiDAR range measured from the round-trip time of a laser pulse.
  • Doppler shift — frequency shift in a radar return proportional to relative radial velocity; radar's unique superpower.
  • 4D imaging radar — newer radar with elevation resolution added to range/azimuth/velocity, closing the gap to LiDAR-like density.
  • Extrinsics / intrinsics — extrinsics = sensor pose relative to the vehicle frame; intrinsics = a sensor's internal calibration (e.g. camera focal length, distortion).
  • Ego-motion compensation — correcting sensor data for the vehicle's own movement during a scan or exposure window.

Self-test: why not just add more cameras and skip LiDAR/radar entirely?

Camera-only stacks (e.g. Tesla's approach) are viable but push enormous burden onto learned depth and temporal geometry — they must infer metric 3D from motion parallax and priors, which degrades in featureless or adversarial scenes and has no direct velocity signal. Radar/LiDAR give you signals that are physically direct rather than inferred, which matters most exactly in the rare, safety-critical tail cases a camera-only net hasn't seen enough of.

2. Per-modality encoders: how each stream becomes features

Images: CNN vs. ViT

A CNN (e.g. ResNet) builds features through local convolutions with a growing receptive field — strong inductive bias for translation invariance and locality, so it's data-efficient and fast, but its receptive field grows only gradually with depth.

A Vision Transformer (ViT) throws away the convolutional inductive bias and instead: cuts the image into fixed-size patches, linearly projects each patch into a token, adds a positional embedding, and runs full self-attention across all tokens from layer one — so every token can attend to every other token immediately, giving global receptive field at the cost of needing more data (or distillation/pretraining) to learn what convolution got for free.

$$x \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C} \;\rightarrow\; \{p_1, \dots, p_N\},\; p_i \in \mathbb{R}^{P^2 C} \;\rightarrow\; z_i = E p_i + E_{pos,i} \in \mathbb{R}^{D}$$

where the image is split into N = HW/P² patches of size P×P, each patch is flattened and linearly projected by a learned matrix E, and a learned positional embedding E_pos is added so the transformer — which is otherwise permutation-invariant — knows where each patch came from.

At AV scale: CNNs (or CNN backbones like ResNet/RegNet) remain common for their compute efficiency at high resolution and real-time latency budgets; ViT-family backbones dominate when you can afford the compute and want a token representation that fuses cleanly with other modalities (transformers are the natural fusion substrate — see §3).

Point clouds: PointNet, voxelization, PointPillars, range-view

A raw LiDAR point cloud is an unordered set of points — there's no canonical order, so any encoder must be permutation-invariant.

PointNet's core trick: run a shared MLP independently on every point to lift it into a high-dim feature, then apply a symmetric function — max-pooling across the point dimension — to aggregate into one order-independent global feature. Max is symmetric because max(a,b) = max(b,a) regardless of input order; the network never has to care what order the points arrived in.

$$f(\{x_1,\dots,x_n\}) \approx \gamma\big(\max_i\, h(x_i)\big)$$

Three practical ways to turn a point cloud into something a conv/transformer backbone can consume:

  • Voxelization — bin points into a regular 3D grid, aggregate points per voxel (e.g. via a small PointNet), then run 3D or sparse convolutions over the voxel grid. Preserves 3D structure but is compute-heavy — 3D conv scales with the cube of resolution.
  • PointPillars — collapse the z-axis: bin points into vertical columns ("pillars") over a 2D BEV grid, encode each pillar with a PointNet-style MLP+max-pool, scatter the result into a 2D pseudo-image, then run ordinary fast 2D convolutions. This won for real-time AV use because it swaps expensive 3D convolution for cheap 2D convolution while keeping enough geometric fidelity for driving-relevant objects (which are mostly ground-supported, so z-collapse loses little).
  • Range-view — project points onto the native 2D sensor grid the LiDAR actually scans in (range × azimuth), giving a dense image-like representation for free (no binning/sparsity issues) at the cost of scale distortion and occlusion artifacts near object boundaries.
Radar: point-based vs. tensor-based

Sparse radar returns (a handful of points per object, each with range/azimuth/velocity) can be encoded the same way as LiDAR points (PointNet-style), but radar's low native resolution makes this noisy. The alternative is to keep the raw range-Doppler-azimuth tensor pre-detection and feed it through a CNN like an image — richer signal but a nonstandard "image" domain requiring bespoke preprocessing and more fusion engineering.

Terms — Per-modality encoders

  • Patch embedding — linear projection of a flattened image patch into a token vector; ViT's analogue of a conv stem.
  • Permutation invariance — an encoder's output should not depend on the input order of an unordered set (points).
  • Symmetric function — an aggregation (max, sum, mean) whose output is unchanged by reordering its inputs; the mechanism PointNet uses for permutation invariance.
  • Sparse convolution — convolution that only computes at occupied voxel locations, exploiting point-cloud sparsity for speed.
  • Pseudo-image — PointPillars' 2D scatter of per-pillar features, shaped like an image so ordinary 2D CNNs apply.
  • Receptive field — the region of input that influences a given output unit; grows locally in CNNs, is global immediately in attention.

3. The unification problem: everything becomes tokens

Tokens as the common currency

Once you have per-modality features, the design question becomes: what is the shared representation that lets a fusion module compare "a patch of image" against "a pillar of LiDAR" against "a radar return"? The modern answer is tokenization — represent every modality as a set of vectors in a common embedding dimension, each carrying its own positional / spatial encoding so the fusion module knows where in the world (or image) that token came from.

Positional encodings for multimodal fusion aren't just "which pixel" — they need to encode 3D spatial location (so a camera token and a LiDAR token that observe the same physical point can be related), which is why calibration (extrinsics/intrinsics) becomes part of the model's input, not just a preprocessing detail.

Bird's-eye-view (BEV) as the AV lingua franca

The dominant unification strategy in modern AV stacks is to lift every modality into a shared bird's-eye-view (BEV) grid: a top-down 2D (or 2.5D) grid over the ground plane where each cell holds a feature vector summarizing "what's here." BEV is natural for driving because planning, prediction, and most cost maps are inherently top-down, and because LiDAR/radar are already metric in this frame — camera is the modality that needs to be "lifted" into it.

Two dominant camera-to-BEV lifting strategies:

  • LSS-style depth-distribution lifting (Lift-Splat-Shoot) — for each image pixel/feature, predict a discrete probability distribution over depth bins, then "splat" the feature into 3D by scattering it along the camera ray weighted by that depth distribution, and pool into BEV grid cells. Intuition: instead of committing to one depth guess per pixel, spread the pixel's contribution across plausible depths, weighted by confidence.
  • BEVFormer-style deformable-attention lifting — start from a learned, empty BEV grid and, for each BEV query cell, use deformable cross-attention to sample and pull relevant features from the camera image feature maps at a handful of learned offsets, rather than explicitly reasoning about a depth distribution. Intuition: let each BEV cell "ask" the images "what do you see here?" via attention, with the geometry (which image regions correspond to this BEV cell) baked in through the camera projection used to seed the sampling offsets.

Terms — Unification & BEV

  • Tokenization — representing input (of any modality) as a set of vectors in a shared embedding space.
  • Positional / spatial encoding — extra signal added to a token so an order-agnostic attention mechanism knows its location.
  • Bird's-eye-view (BEV) — a top-down grid representation where each cell aggregates fused, multimodal features; the shared "canvas" most AV stacks fuse and plan on.
  • Lift-Splat-Shoot (LSS) — camera-to-BEV method: predict per-pixel depth distribution, splat features along the ray, pool into BEV.
  • BEVFormer / deformable attention — camera-to-BEV method: learned BEV queries pull image features via sparse, learned sampling offsets instead of explicit depth prediction.
  • Occupancy grid — a 3D (or BEV) generalization of detection output: per-cell "is something here" probability, useful for open-set/unlabeled obstacles.
Related: §4 fusion, §7 world models
Self-test: why is BEV preferred over fusing directly in each camera's own image plane?

Different cameras (and LiDAR/radar) each have their own viewpoint and distortion; fusing in any single sensor's native frame privileges that sensor's geometry and makes it awkward to combine multi-camera coverage or reason metrically. BEV is viewpoint-agnostic and metric, aligns naturally with planning/map representations, and lets every sensor contribute to the same physical grid regardless of where it's mounted.

4. Fusion strategies

Early, mid, and late fusion

Early fusion combines raw or near-raw sensor data before feature extraction (e.g. painting LiDAR points with per-pixel camera class scores, or concatenating raw modalities into one input tensor). Mid / feature-level fusion extracts features per modality independently, then combines the feature representations (tokens, BEV grids) inside the network — this is where most modern architectures live. Late fusion runs a full independent detection pipeline per modality and merges only the final outputs (e.g. box-level NMS across modality-specific detections).

StrategyBandwidth / computeFailure isolationLearned cross-modal interaction
EarlyHigh (raw data volume, tight sync needed)Poor — one bad sensor corrupts the shared inputMaximum in principle, but hard to train well
Mid / featureModerateGood — a degraded modality still contributes partial featuresStrong — the model learns to weigh/combine features (e.g. cross-attention)
LateLowBest — independent pipelines, easy to drop oneWeak — combination logic is typically simple (NMS, voting), no learned interaction
How to think about it: the fusion-level choice is a bandwidth/robustness/expressiveness triangle. Interviewers probe this by asking what happens if one sensor is degraded — mid fusion is the usual answer because it's the sweet spot: rich learned interaction without full dependence on any single sensor's raw stream.
Cross-attention as the mid-fusion mechanism

The dominant way to let one modality's tokens query another's is cross-attention: one modality supplies the queries (Q), the other supplies keys and values (K, V).

$$\text{CrossAttn}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V$$

Concretely: if camera BEV queries attend into LiDAR features, then Q comes from the camera-derived BEV tokens (asking "what geometry is here?") and K, V come from LiDAR tokens (answering with precise depth/geometry). The softmax over QKᵀ produces attention weights that decide, per query, how much of each LiDAR token to pull in — this is what lets the model learn, e.g., to trust LiDAR more in a region where camera is glare-blown and trust camera more where LiDAR is sparse.

Three concrete ways to combine tokens once you're in a shared space, from crudest to most expressive:

  • Token concatenation — just concatenate the token sequences from each modality and let the fusion transformer's self-attention sort it out. Simple, but the burden of alignment is pushed entirely onto the network.
  • Cross-attention — explicit, directional querying between modalities as above; more inductive bias about who should query whom.
  • BEV-space (element-wise) summation / concatenation — once every modality is already lifted into the same BEV grid, simply sum or channel-concat aligned grid cells. Cheapest, but only works well because BEV lifting already did the hard geometric alignment work upstream.

Terms — Fusion

  • Early / mid / late fusion — fusion stage relative to feature extraction; see table above.
  • Cross-attention — attention where Q comes from one source and K/V from another, letting one modality query another.
  • Query / Key / Value (Q/K/V) — the three learned projections in attention; Q asks, K indexes, V supplies content.
  • Point painting — an early-fusion technique that appends camera-derived semantic scores onto raw LiDAR points before geometric processing.
  • Non-maximum suppression (NMS) — dedup mechanism for overlapping box detections; common late-fusion merge step.
Related: §3 BEV, §5 training
Self-test: interviewer asks "why not just concatenate everything and let self-attention learn it"?

You can, and large-scale multimodal transformers sometimes do — but at AV real-time latency budgets, concatenation loses the strong geometric prior that a targeted cross-attention (query = "what's at this exact 3D location") gives you, so it typically needs far more data/compute to reach the same accuracy. Structured cross-attention with BEV-anchored queries is a way of hand-feeding the model a good inductive bias, trading a bit of generality for efficiency and faster convergence.

5. Training the encoder

Supervised heads

The fused BEV (or token) representation feeds task-specific heads: 3D detection (boxes + class + velocity), semantic/instance segmentation, and occupancy prediction (dense per-cell occupied/free/unknown, increasingly preferred over boxes because it handles arbitrary and open-set obstacle shapes).

Self-supervised and contrastive pretraining

Masked autoencoding (MAE) per modality: mask out a large fraction of patches/points/pillars and train the encoder-decoder to reconstruct them — forces the encoder to learn structure without labels. Works per-modality (masked image patches, masked LiDAR voxels) and can be extended cross-modally (mask one modality, reconstruct it from the others).

Contrastive image-text alignment (CLIP-style) trains an image encoder and a text encoder jointly so that matched image-text pairs have high cosine similarity and mismatched pairs have low similarity, via the InfoNCE loss:

$$\mathcal{L} = -\log \frac{\exp(\text{sim}(v_i, t_i)/\tau)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N} \exp(\text{sim}(v_i, t_j)/\tau)}$$

where sim is typically cosine similarity, the sum runs over one positive pair and N−1 negatives in the batch, and temperature τ controls how sharply the softmax concentrates probability mass — a low τ makes the loss punish near-miss negatives harder, sharpening the learned embedding space at the cost of training stability. For AV, this style of contrastive pretraining is used to align sensor tokens (e.g. LiDAR point features) with language/vision foundation-model embeddings, which is part of what enables open-vocabulary perception and feeds VLM-style driving stacks.

Distillation and multi-task balancing

Knowledge distillation: train a smaller/faster deployable encoder to match the outputs (or intermediate features) of a large, offline "teacher" model that may use more sensors, more compute, or even future/offline-only information (e.g. a teacher with access to a denser LiDAR sweep). Multi-task balancing matters because detection, segmentation, and occupancy losses have different scales and gradient magnitudes — naive summed loss lets one task dominate; common fixes are uncertainty-based task weighting, gradient normalization (e.g. GradNorm), or simple manually-tuned loss weights validated empirically.

Terms — Training

  • Masked autoencoding (MAE) — self-supervised pretraining via masking-and-reconstruction.
  • InfoNCE loss — contrastive loss maximizing similarity of positive pairs vs. in-batch negatives.
  • Temperature (τ) — softmax sharpness parameter in contrastive losses.
  • Knowledge distillation — training a student model to match a stronger teacher's outputs/features.
  • Multi-task loss balancing — weighting scheme so no single task's loss dominates joint training (e.g. uncertainty weighting, GradNorm).
  • Occupancy prediction — dense free/occupied/unknown grid task, handles open-set obstacles that boxes can't.
Related: §6 robustness, §7 modern context
Self-test: why pretrain with MAE/contrastive objectives at all if you have labeled detection data?

Labeled 3D detection/segmentation data is expensive and comparatively scarce; self-supervised objectives can use the much larger pool of unlabeled sensor logs to learn general-purpose structure (geometry, semantics) before fine-tuning on the smaller labeled set — improving sample efficiency and often robustness to distribution shift.

6. Robustness & production concerns

What breaks in the real world
  • Sensor dropout training — randomly zero out or drop entire modalities during training so the fused model doesn't collapse when a real sensor fails or is occluded (mud on a lens, a dead LiDAR channel); this is the training-time mechanism that makes the "what if you lose LiDAR?" interview question answerable with "the model was trained for it."
  • Calibration / extrinsics drift — sensor mounts shift slightly over time/vibration/temperature; if BEV lifting relies on fixed extrinsics, drift silently misaligns fused features. Production systems need online recalibration or drift-tolerant fusion (e.g. attention that doesn't hard-code exact geometric offsets).
  • Temporal fusion — a single frame is noisy and occlusion-prone; AV stacks fuse a sliding window of past BEV features (or a recurrent/streaming memory, as in BEVFormer's temporal self-attention) to smooth detections and infer occluded or momentarily-invisible objects from history.
  • Latency budgeting — a real-time stack has a hard end-to-end deadline (commonly tens of ms); every stage (per-modality encode, fusion, heads) gets an explicit millisecond budget, and architecture choices (PointPillars over voxel 3D-conv, e.g.) are frequently driven by this budget more than by raw accuracy.
  • Uncertainty estimation — safety-critical systems need to know when they don't know; encoders increasingly output calibrated confidence (e.g. per-detection variance, ensembling, or evidential heads) so downstream planning can be conservative under high uncertainty rather than trusting a confidently-wrong detection.
How to think about it: "robustness" in an interview answer should always cash out as a concrete training-time or system-level mechanism (dropout augmentation, temporal windowing, latency budget table, uncertainty head) — not just the word "robust."

Terms — Robustness & production

  • Sensor dropout (modality dropout) — training-time augmentation that zeroes a modality to force graceful degradation.
  • Extrinsics drift — slow miscalibration of sensor mounting pose over vehicle lifetime.
  • Temporal fusion / sliding window — combining BEV features across recent frames for smoother, occlusion-robust perception.
  • Latency budget — the explicit per-stage millisecond allocation within an end-to-end real-time deadline.
  • Uncertainty estimation — calibrated confidence output so downstream planning can react to model doubt.

7. Modern context: feeding VLA/VLM stacks and world models

Tokens don't stop at detection heads

The same tokenized, fused representation that feeds detection/segmentation/occupancy heads is increasingly fed directly into a downstream sequence model — a driving world model that predicts future scene states, or a VLA (vision-language-action)-style policy that maps perception tokens (plus language/goal conditioning) directly to trajectories or control. This is the same architectural move you've already seen in manipulation VLAs: perception encoder → shared token space → transformer backbone → action/prediction head, just with AV-specific sensors and BEV geometry substituted for the robot-arm camera stream. Framing your multimodal encoder answer as "the tokens I produce are exactly what a downstream world-model/policy transformer consumes" is a strong way to connect it to the interviewer's broader system and to your own VLA background.

Terms — Modern context

  • World model — a learned model that predicts future world/scene states conditioned on current tokens and actions.
  • VLA (vision-language-action) — architecture mapping perception (+ language) tokens directly to actions/trajectories.
  • End-to-end driving — architectures that go from sensor tokens to trajectory/control with minimal hand-engineered intermediate stages.
