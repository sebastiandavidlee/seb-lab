Data → Inference: Handling Increasing Complexity

This page is the pipeline story an AV foundation-models interviewer wants to hear end-to-end: as data grows in volume and complexity (§1), you scale models and training strategy to absorb it (§2), and then you have to claw back all that capacity at inference time to hit a latency/memory budget on real hardware (§3). Every stage has a standard vocabulary; you already have hands-on intuition for §3 from AWQ-int4 quantizing world models — this section gives you the formal terms interviewers expect on top of that intuition.

1. Data growth & complexity

Dataset scaling means as you go from thousands to millions to billions of examples, the returns don't come for free — bigger datasets are also messier (label noise, near-duplicates, long tail) and the marginal value of another random example drops (diminishing returns), which is why curation starts to matter more than raw count past a certain scale.

Long-tail distributions: real-world data (object classes, driving scenarios, road-user behaviors) follows a heavy-tailed frequency distribution — a few classes/scenarios dominate the data, and a huge number of rare-but-safety-critical cases (a couch in the road, a person in a wheelchair, an overturned truck) sit in the tail with almost no training examples each. A model trained on i.i.d.-sampled data will systematically underperform on the tail exactly where failure is most costly — this is class imbalance's cousin, but about the input distribution's structure, not just label counts.

Distribution shift is when the deployment-time data distribution differs from the training distribution, and it comes in three formal flavors, distinguished by what's assumed fixed vs. changing between \(P_{train}(x,y)\) and \(P_{test}(x,y)\):

  • Covariate shift: \(P(x)\) changes, but \(P(y\mid x)\) stays the same — e.g. more highway driving at test time than in training, but a stop sign still means the same thing conditioned on pixels.
  • Label shift: \(P(y)\) changes, \(P(x\mid y)\) stays the same — e.g. pedestrian frequency differs between a training city and a deployment city, but pedestrians still look the same given the label.
  • Concept shift: \(P(y\mid x)\) itself changes — the same input now means something different (a road marking's meaning changes with new traffic law, or a construction-zone convention differs by region). This is the hardest to fix by reweighting alone since the mapping itself is wrong, not just its sampling frequency.

Domain gap and domain randomization: domain gap is the distributional mismatch between a synthetic/simulated training domain and the real deployment domain — different lighting statistics, texture realism, sensor noise models, physics fidelity. Domain randomization deliberately widens the synthetic training distribution (randomizing textures, lighting, camera params, physics parameters far beyond what's realistic) so that the real domain falls inside the training distribution's support rather than outside it — you're not trying to match reality exactly, you're trying to make reality one unremarkable sample among many the model already saw. This directly maps onto keypoint/synthetic-data work: if your synthetic renderer's texture/lighting variance is too narrow, you get a domain gap; randomizing those axes aggressively is the standard mitigation before reaching for more expensive sim-to-real transfer methods.

Data curation & active learning: curation is selecting/filtering/deduplicating training data for quality and coverage rather than just scale (removing near-duplicates, balancing the long tail, flagging label noise). Active learning is a targeted version: instead of randomly sampling what to label next, use the current model to identify the examples it's most uncertain about (or most likely to be informative) and prioritize labeling those — cheaper than blindly scaling labeled data, especially for the tail.

Augmentation: synthesizing label-preserving input transformations (crop, color jitter, flip, mixup, cutout, simulated sensor noise) to artificially expand the effective training distribution and reduce variance without collecting more real data — the "free" cousin of domain randomization, applied to real data instead of a simulator.

Covariate/label/concept shift is worth memorizing as a fixed lookup: ask "what's assumed fixed?" — \(P(y|x)\) fixed → covariate; \(P(x|y)\) fixed → label; nothing fixed / the mapping itself moved → concept. Concept shift is the one you can't out-resample your way past.
  • long-tail distribution — heavy-tailed frequency over classes/scenarios; rare cases are underrepresented exactly where failure is costliest. Related: class imbalance (classification page §7), active learning.
  • covariate shift — \(P(x)\) changes, \(P(y|x)\) fixed. Related: label shift, concept shift.
  • label shift — \(P(y)\) changes, \(P(x|y)\) fixed. Related: covariate shift.
  • concept shift — \(P(y|x)\) itself changes; the hardest shift to correct post-hoc. Related: domain gap.
  • domain randomization — widening a simulator's rendering/physics variance so the real domain falls inside training support. Related: sim-to-real, synthetic data.
  • active learning — model-guided selection of which unlabeled examples to label next, prioritizing uncertainty/informativeness. Related: data curation.
A perception model trained mostly in California sun starts missing pedestrians in Pittsburgh winter. Is this covariate, label, or concept shift?Most likely covariate shift — the input distribution \(P(x)\) changed (snow, different lighting/contrast statistics) but a pedestrian in snow still is a pedestrian conditioned on the true visual content; \(P(y|x)\) is unchanged even though the model's learned decision boundary, tuned to sunny-domain statistics, fails to generalize.
Why doesn't reweighting/resampling fix concept shift the way it fixes label shift?Resampling changes which examples you see or how much they're weighted, which corrects a mismatched prior \(P(y)\); it cannot fix a case where the actual mapping \(P(y|x)\) has changed, since no amount of reweighting existing (x,y) pairs recovers a mapping that's now wrong for new data.

2. Model scaling

Capacity vs. data — scaling-law intuition: empirically (Kaplan et al. 2020, refined by Chinchilla/Hoffmann et al. 2022), test loss on large language/vision-language models follows a smooth power-law decay as a function of model parameters \(N\), dataset size \(D\), and compute \(C\), each holding the others non-bottlenecked. The Chinchilla finding specifically was that prior large models (GPT-3-era) were over-parameterized relative to their training data — compute-optimal training scales \(N\) and \(D\) roughly together (both should grow at similar rates as compute increases), not \(N\) alone. The practical takeaway for an interview: "bigger model" and "more data" are not substitutes, they're jointly constrained by a compute budget, and there's a compute-optimal ratio between them rather than a free lunch from scaling either alone.

Pretraining → fine-tuning: pretrain a large model on a broad, cheaply-available objective (next-token prediction, masked reconstruction, contrastive matching) over massive weakly-labeled or unlabeled data, then fine-tune (continue training, usually with a much smaller learning rate and much less data) on the specific labeled downstream task. This decouples "learn general structure of the world" (data-hungry, label-cheap) from "specialize to my task" (label-expensive, but needs far less data because the representation is already good).

Self-supervised / contrastive pretraining: self-supervised learning derives its own supervisory signal from the unlabeled data's structure (predict a masked patch, predict the next token, match two augmented views of the same image). Contrastive learning is a specific self-supervised family (SimCLR, CLIP, MoCo): pull representations of two "positive" views (augmentations of the same underlying instance, or a matched image-text pair) together in embedding space, and push apart representations of "negative" (unrelated) pairs, typically via the InfoNCE loss:

$$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{InfoNCE}} = -\log \frac{\exp(\text{sim}(z_i, z_j^+)/\tau)}{\sum_{k} \exp(\text{sim}(z_i, z_k)/\tau)} $$

where \(\text{sim}\) is typically cosine similarity, \(z_j^+\) is the positive match, the sum in the denominator runs over one positive and many negatives, and \(\tau\) is a temperature controlling how sharply the model is pushed to separate hard negatives — structurally this is just softmax + cross-entropy again (§3 on the classification page), applied to a "which one is the true match" classification problem over similarity scores instead of class logits.

Transfer learning is the umbrella term for reusing representations learned on one task/domain to accelerate or improve learning on another — pretrain→fine-tune is the dominant modern instance of it, but it also covers reusing frozen backbone features across tasks (linear probing), or multi-task training where a shared encoder feeds several heads.

When someone says "scaling laws," they mean an empirical power-law curve fit to loss-vs-(N,D,C), not a theoretical guarantee — it's a planning tool for compute-allocation decisions, not a proof that bigger always wins on every downstream metric.
  • scaling laws — empirical power-law relation between test loss and (params, data, compute). Related: Chinchilla compute-optimal ratio.
  • compute-optimal training — the Chinchilla finding that N and D should scale together under a fixed compute budget, not N alone. Related: scaling laws.
  • self-supervised learning (SSL) — supervisory signal derived from unlabeled data's own structure. Related: contrastive learning, masked pretraining.
  • contrastive learning / InfoNCE — pull positive-pair embeddings together, push negatives apart; structurally a softmax classification over similarity scores. Related: cross-entropy (classification page §3), CLIP.
  • fine-tuning — continued training of a pretrained model on a smaller, task-specific labeled set, usually low LR. Related: LoRA (parameter-efficient fine-tuning).
  • linear probing — freezing a pretrained backbone and training only a lightweight head; measures representation quality directly. Related: transfer learning.
Chinchilla found earlier LLMs were "undertrained" relative to their size. What does that mean operationally, and what would you do differently with a fixed compute budget?It means models like GPT-3 had more parameters than their training-token count justified for compute-optimality — for a fixed compute budget, better test loss is achieved with a smaller model trained on proportionally more tokens rather than maxing out parameter count alone; the practical move is to shrink N and grow D together rather than scaling N in isolation.
Why is the InfoNCE loss "structurally the same" as softmax cross-entropy?Both compute a normalized exponential score over a set of candidates (class logits vs. similarity scores against one positive + many negatives) and take the negative log of the true/matching candidate's normalized probability — InfoNCE is softmax+CE where the "classes" are the negative samples in the batch and the "true class" is the known positive pair.

3. Making it fast at inference

Training-time capacity has to be paid back at inference time against a latency, memory, and (for real-time AV stacks) power budget. The standard optimization menu, roughly in order of "how much do you have to change about the model":

Mixed precision: run matmuls in a lower-precision format (fp16/bf16 instead of fp32) while keeping a master fp32 copy of weights and loss-scaling for gradient underflow — this is primarily a training-time speedup (bf16/fp16 tensor cores are 2-8x faster than fp32 on modern GPUs, halves memory bandwidth), but the same lower-precision inference path also directly cuts inference latency and memory, and is usually the first, free-est lever before touching quantization.

Quantization reduces numeric precision of weights (and optionally activations) below fp16, most commonly to int8 or int4, mapping a float range to a small integer range via a scale (and optionally zero-point) factor. Two axes matter:

  • PTQ (post-training quantization) vs. QAT (quantization-aware training): PTQ quantizes an already-trained model using only a small calibration dataset to estimate activation/weight ranges — no retraining, cheap, fast, but accuracy loss grows as you push to lower bit-widths (especially int4 with naive PTQ). QAT simulates quantization during training/fine-tuning (fake-quant ops that round-then-dequantize in the forward pass, gradients estimated through the rounding op via a straight-through estimator) so the model's weights adapt to the quantization noise — more expensive (needs training infra and data) but recovers most of the accuracy loss at aggressive bit-widths.
  • Weight-only vs. weight+activation quantization: weight-only quantization (GPTQ, AWQ) keeps activations in fp16/bf16 and only quantizes the weights to int4/int8; weight+activation quantization (SmoothQuant-style) quantizes both. Weight-only wins on quality-per-bit for LLM/large-model inference because weight distributions are comparatively well-behaved (roughly Gaussian, few outliers) while activations frequently have per-channel outlier values that blow up quantization error if forced into low-bit integers — quantizing only the well-behaved tensor avoids the worst error source. AWQ specifically observes that not all weights matter equally: it identifies "salient" weight channels via the magnitude of their corresponding activations (large-activation channels amplify whatever weight error sits there), and protects those channels by per-channel scaling before quantizing, so the channels that matter most for output error see effectively higher precision even though the stored format is still int4 uniformly. This is exactly the mechanism behind int4 weight-only quantization staying close to bf16 quality despite the aggressive bit-width — you already have this from hands-on AWQ-int4 world-model work; the formal names for what you did are "weight-only PTQ with activation-aware salient-channel scaling."

Since weight-only quantization leaves activations (and thus the matmul compute itself) in higher precision, its speedup mostly comes from reduced memory bandwidth (fewer bytes moved to load weights) rather than from faster arithmetic — which is why it helps most in memory-bandwidth-bound regimes (see the edge-deployment note below), and less in compute-bound regimes with very large batch sizes.

Pruning removes weights judged unimportant. Unstructured pruning zeroes individual weights anywhere in a tensor based on some importance criterion (e.g. magnitude) — can reach very high sparsity with minimal accuracy loss, but the resulting sparse tensor doesn't map to a dense speedup on standard hardware without specialized sparse kernels or hardware support (e.g. NVIDIA's 2:4 structured-sparsity tensor cores). Structured pruning removes whole structural units — channels, filters, attention heads, even entire layers — so the resulting model is a smaller dense model that runs faster on ordinary hardware with no special kernel support, at the cost of typically needing higher sparsity budgets or fine-tuning to recover the same accuracy as unstructured pruning would at an equivalent nominal sparsity level.

Knowledge distillation: train a smaller "student" model to match a larger "teacher" model's output distribution (soft labels/logits, often temperature-scaled — the same temperature-scaling mechanism from calibration, but here used to soften the teacher's distribution so it carries more inter-class information than a one-hot label would) rather than training the student from scratch on hard labels alone. The soft targets carry "dark knowledge" — relative confidences across wrong classes — that hard labels discard, letting a much smaller student recover more of the teacher's performance than training on ground truth alone would achieve at that size.

Compilation / kernel fusion: compilers like TensorRT, TVM, or torch.compile fuse sequences of elementwise/normalization/activation ops (e.g. conv → batchnorm → ReLU) into a single GPU kernel, eliminating the intermediate memory round-trips (write conv output to HBM, read it back for batchnorm, write again, read again for ReLU) that dominate latency for small, memory-bound ops. This is a "free" optimization in the sense that it changes nothing about the model's math or accuracy — pure execution-graph rewriting for the target hardware.

Batching, latency vs. throughput: larger batch sizes amortize fixed per-call overhead and improve hardware utilization, raising throughput (examples/sec) — but every request in a batch waits for the slowest, so per-request latency rises with batch size. Real-time AV inference is almost always latency-constrained (a perception result late by 50ms is often worse than useless), which pushes toward small batches or batch-size-1, trading away throughput headroom that a batch-serving system (e.g. an offline-labeling pipeline) would happily use. Continuous/dynamic batching (as used in LLM-serving systems) partially reconciles this by admitting new requests into an in-flight batch as others finish, instead of waiting for a fixed batch to fully complete.

Edge-deployment constraints — memory bandwidth as the real bottleneck: on edge/embedded accelerators, inference for typical (not enormous-batch) workloads is very often memory-bandwidth-bound, not compute-bound: the GPU/accelerator's arithmetic units sit idle waiting for weights and activations to be streamed in from (V)RAM, because modern accelerators' FLOP/s grew much faster than their memory bandwidth over the last decade (the so-called "memory wall"). This is the single most important framing for why quantization helps as much as it does at inference: even though int4 weight-only quantization doesn't necessarily speed up the arithmetic (it may even require a dequantize-then-multiply step), it directly cuts the number of bytes that must be streamed from memory per weight, which is precisely the bottleneck resource in this regime — this is also why weight-only (not weight+activation) quantization is often sufficient: the activations aren't the memory-bandwidth cost driver at small batch sizes, the (repeatedly reloaded, large) weight matrix is.

Monitoring / eval in production: shipping is not the end — production systems need drift detection (statistically monitoring whether live input distributions or prediction distributions have shifted away from the validation baseline — connects directly back to §1's distribution shift), shadow deployment (running a new model alongside the production model on live traffic without acting on its outputs, to compare before cutover), canary rollout (routing a small traffic percentage to the new model with automatic rollback triggers), and calibration monitoring (re-checking reliability diagrams periodically, since calibration can silently drift even when accuracy looks stable).

The compact interview answer to "how do you make a big model fast": mixed precision first (near-free), then quantize weights (int8 safe, int4 usually needs AWQ/GPTQ-style protection or QAT to hold quality), then structured-prune or distill if you need an actually smaller architecture (not just smaller numbers), then compile/fuse kernels for the target hardware, and tune batch size to the latency budget you actually have — and always sanity-check against whether you're compute-bound or bandwidth-bound before picking which lever matters most.

  • PTQ (post-training quantization) — quantize a trained model with calibration data only, no retraining. Related: QAT, AWQ, GPTQ.
  • QAT (quantization-aware training) — simulate quantization noise during training via fake-quant + straight-through estimator, so weights adapt. Related: PTQ.
  • weight-only quantization — quantize weights, keep activations higher-precision; wins because weight distributions are outlier-light and memory bandwidth (not compute) is the bottleneck. Related: AWQ, memory-bandwidth bound.
  • AWQ (activation-aware weight quantization) — protects salient weight channels (identified via activation magnitude) with per-channel scaling before int4 quantization. Related: GPTQ, weight-only quantization.
  • structured vs. unstructured pruning — remove whole channels/heads (dense speedup, no special kernels) vs. individual weights (higher sparsity, needs sparse-kernel hardware support). Related: L1 (classification page §5).
  • knowledge distillation — train a small student to match a large teacher's (temperature-softened) output distribution. Related: temperature scaling (classification page §8).
  • kernel fusion — compile multiple ops into one kernel to remove intermediate memory round-trips (TensorRT-style). Related: memory-bandwidth bound.
  • memory-bandwidth-bound — inference regime where arithmetic units wait on data movement, not compute; the reason quantization helps latency even without speeding up math. Related: edge deployment, weight-only quantization.
  • latency vs. throughput — per-request response time vs. aggregate examples/sec; batch size trades one for the other. Related: continuous batching.
  • drift detection / shadow deployment / canary rollout — production monitoring and safe-rollout patterns for catching distribution shift and regressions post-launch. Related: distribution shift (§1).
Why does int4 weight-only quantization (AWQ) hold quality so much better than naively quantizing both weights and activations to int4?Activations have per-channel outliers that blow up under uniform low-bit quantization, while weights are comparatively well-behaved; keeping activations in fp16/bf16 avoids that error source entirely, and AWQ additionally protects the specific weight channels that pair with large-activation channels via per-channel scaling, so the channels contributing most to output error effectively retain more precision.
You quantize a model to int4 and see almost no inference speedup despite a 4x reduction in model size on disk. What's the most likely explanation?The workload was compute-bound rather than memory-bandwidth-bound at the batch size you tested (e.g. very large batch, or activations not actually quantized so the matmul still runs at higher precision) — weight-only quantization's main benefit is reduced bytes moved from memory, which only translates to latency wins when memory bandwidth, not arithmetic throughput, was the bottleneck.
Structured pruning at 50% sparsity typically loses more accuracy than unstructured pruning at 50% sparsity. Why would you ever choose structured over unstructured?Because unstructured sparsity doesn't produce a speedup on standard dense hardware/kernels without specialized sparse-compute support — a "50% sparse but still stored/computed dense" model gets none of the latency benefit, while structured pruning produces an actually smaller dense model that runs faster everywhere, which is usually what an inference deployment needs even at the cost of some extra accuracy recovery effort (fine-tuning).
