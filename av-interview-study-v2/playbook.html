The 45-Minute System-Design Playbook

The Encoder page is what to say; this page is when and how. System-design rounds are graded on process — whether you clarify before you architect, name trade-offs unprompted, and respect budgets — at least as much as on the design itself. Run the arc below until the timing is muscle memory.

1. The seven-step arc

2. What to actually say at each step

Step 1 — Clarify the problem (4 min)

Thinking pattern: shrink the problem space before touching it. Ask the forking questions — the ones whose answers change the design. Ask: “What's the operational domain — highway, urban, both? What's the sensor suite — am I choosing it or is it fixed? Is this for a modular stack (boxes to a planner) or end-to-end? What compute class — automotive SoC or datacenter-in-the-trunk?” One-liner: “Before I draw anything: fixed sensor suite or am I choosing it?”

Step 2 — Define I/O and metrics (5 min)

Thinking pattern: a system is its interface plus its score. State inputs (6 cameras, 1 LiDAR, 5 radars, ego-motion, calibration), outputs (BEV features → 3D boxes with velocity + uncertainty, occupancy, map elements), and how you'll know it works: mAP/NDS for detection, IoU for occupancy, and — say this — closed-loop and safety-weighted metrics, because offline mAP doesn't price a missed pedestrian differently from a missed cone. One-liner: “Success is not mAP; it's p99 latency under budget with no safety-critical class regressing.”

Step 3 — Data story (5 min)

Thinking pattern: volume is free, labels and the long tail are not. Fleet logs give unlabeled scale (→ self-supervised pretraining); auto-labeling with offboard privileged models makes cheap pseudo-labels; humans label the hard slice; synthetic data and domain randomization cover the long tail you can't collect (your keypoint/synthetic-data work is evidence here — see Section 5). Name curriculum: mine hard examples with the model in the loop. One-liner: “The data engine matters more than the architecture after month three.”

Step 4 — Architecture + trade-offs (13 min)

Thinking pattern: draw left to right, narrate every arrow, and attach a trade-off to every choice as you make it — don't wait to be asked. This is the encoder-page centerpiece diagram: branches → tokens → fusion → BEV → heads. Announce alternatives you rejected: “I fuse mid-level rather than late because box-level fusion can't recover sub-threshold evidence; I keep a late-fused fallback for safety.” One-liner: “Every arrow I draw, I'll tell you what it costs.”

Step 5 — Training strategy (7 min)

Thinking pattern: maturity ladder, matching Encoder Section 7. Supervised heads first; MAE-style pretraining on fleet images/voxels; contrastive cross-modal alignment; distillation from an offboard teacher; then the multi-task balancing risk and how you'd watch for negative transfer. One-liner: “Pretrain on the fleet, fine-tune on the labels, distill into the budget.”

Step 6 — Inference budget (5 min)

Thinking pattern: allocate the 100 ms explicitly (the latency table), name the levers per stage — resolution, quantization, sparse attention, head pruning — and insist on p99 not mean. This is your strongest home turf: quantifying a world-model quantization win (int4-resident, near-BF16, 18× faster) is exactly the currency of this step. One-liner: “I budget p99, and the image backbones are where the milliseconds are.”

Step 7 — Failure modes + monitoring (4 min)

Thinking pattern: enumerate by category, not anecdote: sensor loss (dropout training → graceful degradation), calibration drift (online self-calibration + cross-modal residual monitoring), distribution shift (new city, weather), rare classes (open-vocabulary head, data engine), and silent regressions (per-slice evaluation, shadow mode). One-liner: “The system should know when it doesn't know — uncertainty is an output.”

Memorize these terms

operational design domain (ODD) forking question closed-loop metric data engine auto-labeling / offboard model hard-example mining shadow mode per-slice evaluation

Quiz: You have 45 minutes and the interviewer says "design a perception encoder for our AVs." What are your first two sentences?

Something like: “Before drawing anything, let me pin the constraints: is the sensor suite fixed — cameras, LiDAR, radar — and is this feeding a modular planner or an end-to-end stack? And what's the compute class onboard?” Clarifying questions first — the answers fork the whole design, and asking them is the graded behavior.

Quiz: Why is stating metrics before architecture worth more points than a fancier architecture?

Because architecture can't be evaluated without a score to optimize: the metric choice determines every later trade-off (e.g., a p99 latency bound rules out heavy ensembles; safety-weighted recall changes threshold tuning). Interviewers read metrics-first as evidence you've shipped systems, not just read papers.

3. Probe trainer — the 8 follow-ups they will actually ask

Interviewers steer the round with probes. Each card gives the reasoning pattern — the shape of a good answer, not a script to recite. Click to reveal, then answer aloud before reading.

“What happens if you lose LiDAR mid-drive?”

Pattern: degrade, don't die. (1) Detection: health monitor flags the dead branch. (2) Mechanism: fusion was trained with sensor dropout, so attention renormalizes over camera+radar tokens; depth now comes from learned camera depth priors + radar ranging — noisier, so uncertainty widens. (3) System response: uncertainty heads report it, planner gets wider margins / reduced ODD (slower speed, larger following distance). Never claim unchanged performance — claim known, bounded degradation.

Covers: Encoder §8 (dropout, uncertainty), §6 (failure isolation)

“You get 10× more data. What changes?”

Pattern: ask what KIND of data, then re-balance the pipeline. 10× unlabeled fleet logs → scale self-supervised pretraining (MAE, world-model objectives) and mine the long tail; labels are now the bottleneck → invest in auto-labeling. 10× labels → bigger backbone becomes trainable (ViTs love data), re-check that the model, not the data, is the ceiling. Either way: dedup and curate — data value is heavy-tailed, and 10× of the same commute is nearly worthless.

Covers: Encoder §7 (pretraining ladder), step 3 (data engine)

“Halve your latency budget. Go.”

Pattern: profile first, then spend cheapest accuracy first. Order the levers by accuracy-per-millisecond: quantization (INT8/FP8 — often near-free), input resolution on far-field-irrelevant cameras, token pruning / sparser deformable attention, then distill the big model into a smaller trunk (keep the teacher offboard). Only after those: drop heads or modalities. Give real numbers if you have them — a quantization result with measured speedup and quality delta is worth ten adjectives.

Covers: Encoder §8b (latency table), §7 (distillation)

“Your model is confidently wrong on a rare class. What do you do?”

Pattern: separate the calibration problem from the coverage problem. Confidently = miscalibration → temperature scaling, better uncertainty heads, ensembles for epistemic signal. Wrong on rare = coverage → data engine: mine similar cases from the fleet (embedding search), auto-label, synthesize the long tail, retrain, and add the class to per-slice eval so it can't silently regress again. Close the loop: this failure becomes a monitored slice forever.

Covers: Encoder §8 (uncertainty), step 3 + step 7

“Sensor calibration drifts over the vehicle's life. How does your design cope?”

Pattern: tolerate small, detect medium, refuse large. Tolerate: calibration-jitter augmentation in training so small errors don't matter. Detect: online self-calibration from ego-motion and cross-modal residuals (LiDAR edges vs image edges) as a continuous health metric. Refuse: past a threshold, flag the vehicle for service and shrink the ODD. Name the failure signature: long range degrades first, because angular error scales with range.

Covers: Encoder §8a (extrinsics drift), §4 (projection dependence)

“How do you know your new encoder is actually better?”

Pattern: layered evaluation, offline → shadow → closed-loop. Offline: same data, per-slice metrics (night, rain, rare classes, far range) — aggregate mAP hides regressions. Shadow mode: run the candidate in parallel on the fleet, diff its outputs against the incumbent on live data, human-review the biggest disagreements. Closed-loop: sim/resim evaluation where the planner consumes the new perception, because perception deltas only matter through behavior. Ship gate: no safety-critical slice regresses, p99 within budget.

Covers: step 2 (metrics), step 7 (monitoring)

“New vehicle platform, different sensor positions, day one. Cold start?”

Pattern: design so geometry is config, not weights. Because the encoder consumes calibration explicitly (projection in the lifting/fusion steps) and tokens carry position embeddings derived from it, new extrinsics are mostly a config change; camera-height/FOV shift is a domain gap → fine-tune on a small new-platform dataset + augment old data by resampling to the new geometry + sim of the new rig. What breaks worst: anything that memorized the old rig implicitly — which is the argument for geometry-explicit designs in the first place.

Covers: Encoder §4 (calibration-explicit lifting), §2 (tokens as interface)

“Long-tail pedestrian behavior — someone crawling, in costume, on a scooter. Your answer can't be 'collect more data.'”

Pattern: shrink reliance on class labels. (1) Occupancy/geometry path: an occupancy head doesn't care what it is — something is there, don't hit it; this is the safety net that must not depend on classification. (2) Open-vocabulary semantics: VLM-aligned features recognize "person-like" beyond the label set. (3) Synthetic long tail: generate the crawl/costume cases (your DR/synthetic-data experience is the evidence). (4) Uncertainty → behavior: low-confidence dynamic object near the road = slow down regardless of class.

Covers: Encoder §9 (open-vocab, world models), §8 (uncertainty)

6. Anti-patterns — the four ways strong candidates fail

Architecture-first. Jumping to "I'd use BEVFormer with…" in minute two. Reads as pattern-matching, not design. Fix: spend the first four minutes asking forking questions; earn the diagram.
No metrics. A design with no definition of better. Everything after it is unfalsifiable. Fix: write the score on the board at minute 5 and point at it for every trade-off.
No constraint questions. Designing for an unstated compute/sensor/ODD budget — 50% chance you solved the wrong problem. Fix: "fixed suite or my choice?" costs ten seconds and forks everything.
Silent trade-offs. Making reasonable choices without naming the rejected alternative — the interviewer can't award points for reasoning they never heard. Fix: the sentence template "X over Y, because Z, at the cost of W" after every arrow.

7. The one-page checklist (print this)

45-minute run-through — check each line aloud

  1. 0:00 Clarify (4') — ODD? sensor suite fixed? modular or end-to-end? compute class? offline or online?
  2. 4:00 I/O + metrics (5') — inputs w/ rates; outputs w/ uncertainty; per-slice mAP + occ IoU + p99 latency; "closed-loop is the real score."
  3. 9:00 Data (5') — fleet logs → self-sup; auto-label; human hard-slice; synthetic long tail; hard-example mining loop.
  4. 14:00 Architecture (13') — draw: 3 branches → per-modality encoders (ViT / pillars / MLP) → shared tokens (+modality, +position) → cross-attention w/ BEV queries (say who is Q, who is K/V) → BEV grid (+temporal window) → heads (det+vel+σ, occupancy, forecast). Name the rejected alternative at every arrow (early/late fusion, voxels, LSS-vs-queries).
  5. 27:00 Training (7') — MAE pretrain → supervised heads → contrastive alignment → distill from offboard teacher → multi-task balancing (per-task val curves).
  6. 34:00 Inference (5') — stage-by-stage ms table; levers: quantization, resolution, sparse attention; p99 not mean; drop your quantization number here.
  7. 39:00 Failures (4') — sensor dropout ✓ trained-in; calibration monitor; per-slice eval + shadow mode; uncertainty as an output; occupancy as the class-free safety net.
  8. 43:00 Close (2') — "tokens are an interface: detection today, language/policy/world-model heads tomorrow."

8. Proof the arc transfers: "design a classification system that scales"

Same seven steps, different noun — run it in seven one-liners to convince yourself the arc is the skill, not the AV content:

  1. Clarify: how many classes, how imbalanced, does the label set grow over time, latency/throughput target?
  2. I/O + metrics: input distribution and output taxonomy; per-class recall at fixed precision — never accuracy on imbalanced data.
  3. Data: label budget → pretrain self-supervised on everything, fine-tune on the labeled slice, active-learn the boundary cases.
  4. Architecture: pretrained encoder + lightweight head; embedding space designed so new classes are a head change (or k-NN in embedding space), not a retrain.
  5. Training: class-balanced sampling / focal loss; calibrate after training (temperature scaling).
  6. Inference budget: distill/quantize the encoder; batch for throughput or stream for latency — say which.
  7. Failure modes: distribution shift monitor, per-class dashboards, an "unknown/other" escape class, human-in-the-loop review queue for low confidence.
Quiz: What stayed identical between the AV encoder design and the classification system design?

Everything structural: constraints before architecture, metrics as the arbiter of trade-offs, data-engine before model cleverness, explicit budget allocation, and failure modes as designed-in behavior rather than afterthought. Only the domain nouns changed — which is exactly why interviewers grade the arc, not the noun.
