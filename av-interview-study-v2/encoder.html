Designing a Multimodal Perception Encoder

This is the deep page for the known interview question: “design a multimodal perception encoder for an autonomous vehicle.” Work top to bottom — each section is one layer of the answer, and the clickable diagram in Section 2 is the picture you should be able to draw and narrate from memory. The Playbook page covers how to pace this in a 45-minute round.

1. The three sensors and what each one actually sees

Every sensor answer in the interview reduces to this table. Camera is the semantic sensor: dense, cheap, reads text and traffic lights, but depth must be inferred, and it dies in glare, darkness, and rain on the lens. LiDAR is the geometric sensor: direct metric depth via time-of-flight, but sparse (a pedestrian at 60 m may be 4 points), expensive, and degraded by rain/fog/snow scattering. Radar is the physics oddball: it measures radial velocity directly through Doppler shift, punches through weather, and is dirt cheap — but its angular resolution is so coarse that shape and class are nearly unreadable.

Quiz: Which sensor gives you velocity without differentiating positions over time, and why does that matter for a merging-vehicle scenario?

Radar, via Doppler shift on the returned wave. It matters because velocity from cameras/LiDAR requires two or more frames plus association (adding latency and association errors), while radar reports it in a single measurement — so a fast merger is flagged as fast immediately, even before the tracker has converged.

Quiz: A pedestrian at 60 m — what does each sensor give you?

Camera: a small but recognizable patch of pixels (good class evidence, no range). LiDAR: a handful of points (good range, weak class evidence). Radar: possibly nothing, or one low-RCS detection with velocity if they're moving. This is why classification tends to be camera-led and localization LiDAR-led.

Quiz: Why project all modalities into a shared token space before fusing, instead of fusing raw features?

Attention needs its inputs in one vector space of one dimension to compute meaningful dot products. Projecting each modality to a shared d and adding a learned modality embedding lets one fusion transformer treat heterogeneous sensors uniformly — and makes adding or dropping a sensor a change to the token set, not the architecture.

Quiz: Why do task heads hang off the BEV grid rather than off each modality branch?

So every downstream task consumes one fused, metric, ego-centric representation — the heads share computation and stay geometrically consistent with each other (a detection and the occupancy map can't disagree about where things are), and planning can consume BEV outputs directly.

3. Per-modality encoders

3a. Images: patch embedding, written out

A ViT does not see pixels; it sees patch tokens. Take an image \(x \in \mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times 3}\), cut it into non-overlapping \(P\times P\) patches, flatten each into a vector, and multiply by a learned projection:

$$N = \frac{H}{P}\cdot\frac{W}{P}, \qquad x_p^{(i)} \in \mathbb{R}^{P^2\cdot 3}, \qquad z_i = W_e\, x_p^{(i)} + e_i^{\text{pos}}, \quad W_e \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times P^2\cdot 3}$$

Example: a 224×224 image with P = 16 → N = 14 × 14 = 196 tokens; each patch flattens to 16·16·3 = 768 numbers before projection to d.

3b. Point clouds: PointNet's trick, then pillars

A point cloud is an unordered set — feed the same points in a different order and the answer must not change. PointNet achieves this with a symmetric function: embed each point independently with a shared MLP, then max-pool across points:

$$f(\{x_1,\dots,x_N\}) = \gamma\!\Big(\max_{i=1\dots N} h(x_i)\Big)$$

Max is permutation-invariant, so \(f\) is too. The pooled vector is a global shape signature; per-point features come from concatenating it back to each \(h(x_i)\).

Voxels vs pillars. Dense 3D voxelization gives you a regular grid but wastes compute on empty space (a street scene is >90% air), and 3D convolutions are slow. PointPillars collapses the vertical axis: bin points into 2D ground-plane pillars, run a tiny PointNet inside each pillar, and scatter the results into a 2D pseudo-image — after which everything is cheap 2D convolution. That single move (3D problem → 2D backbone) is why pillars won for real-time AV stacks: same family of accuracy, an order of magnitude faster, and it lands natively in BEV, exactly where fusion happens. Sparse 3D convolutions (VoxelNet lineage) close the speed gap when fine vertical structure matters.

Radar encoders. Two regimes: (1) point-level — treat the detection list (x, y, vradial, RCS) like a tiny point cloud and run a PointNet-style MLP; simple and standard. (2) tensor-level — take the pre-CFAR range–azimuth–Doppler cube and run a CNN over it; richer (keeps detections a thresholding step would discard) but heavier and vendor-locked. Say: point-level to ship, tensor-level as the research upgrade.

Quiz: Why max-pool and not sum or mean in PointNet?

All three are permutation-invariant, so any would satisfy the set requirement — but max selects the strongest activation per feature dimension, making the global feature a union of critical points (it provably depends on a small subset of points that outline the shape). Sum/mean dilute with point density, which varies wildly with distance in LiDAR.

Quiz: Give the two reasons PointPillars beat dense voxel nets for production AV perception.

(1) It eliminates 3D convolutions — per-pillar PointNet then 2D convs on a pseudo-image, so it runs at real-time rates on automotive compute. (2) Its output is already a BEV feature map, which is the representation the rest of the stack (fusion, detection heads, planning) wants anyway.

Quiz: What information does the radar tensor have that the detection list doesn't?

Everything below the CFAR detection threshold — weak returns, extended-object structure, and micro-Doppler signatures (e.g., pedestrian leg motion) that the point list has already discarded by thresholding.

4. BEV: lifting perspective into the plan view

Bird's-eye view is the lingua franca of AV perception: a metric, ego-centric, top-down grid (say 200×200 cells at 0.5 m) where LiDAR lives natively, maps are drawn, planning operates, and object scale doesn't change with distance. Cameras are the odd one out — a pixel column is a ray of unknown depth — so the core problem is lifting: moving image features from perspective view into BEV. Two dominant answers:

Trade-off in one breath: LSS is simple and parallel but bets everything on the predicted depth distribution (garbage depth → features land in the wrong cells); BEVFormer avoids explicit depth but leans on accurate calibration for the projection and costs attention compute. Both converge on the same interface — a BEV feature grid — which is the point: choose BEV as the fusion arena, and the camera-lifting method becomes a swappable module.

Say this sentence: “I fuse in BEV because it's metric and ego-centric — LiDAR and radar are already there, planning consumes it directly, and distance-invariant object scale makes detection heads simpler.”

Quiz: Why is camera-to-BEV fundamentally harder than LiDAR-to-BEV?

LiDAR points carry (x, y, z), so mapping to BEV is a lossless coordinate transform. A camera pixel constrains only a ray — inverting projection needs depth, which is unobserved, so any lifting method must either predict depth (LSS) or learn where along the ray to look (query-based attention). It's an ill-posed inverse problem either way.

Quiz: LSS vs BEVFormer — name each one's single point of failure.

LSS: the per-pixel depth distribution — if it's wrong, features are splatted into the wrong BEV cells and nothing downstream can recover. BEVFormer: calibration — the query's projection into the image uses extrinsics/intrinsics, so a miscalibrated camera makes queries sample the wrong pixels (see Section 8 on drift).

5. Cross-attention, with real numbers

Fusion by cross-attention is a lookup: the BEV query asks “who out there is relevant to my cell?”, the sensor tokens answer via dot-product similarity. The single most-tested detail:

$$\text{Attn}(Q,K,V) = \operatorname{softmax}\!\Big(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\Big)V,\qquad \underbrace{Q}_{\text{BEV queries}} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_q\times d},\quad \underbrace{K,V}_{\text{LiDAR tokens}} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_k\times d}$$

Q comes from the side doing the asking (BEV grid); K and V come from the side being consulted (sensor tokens). The √d divisor keeps dot products from saturating softmax as dimension grows.

Reading the demo: Q2 (a cell on top of the parked car) concentrates its mass on the two car tokens — its query vector points the same way their keys do. Q4 (empty road far from everything) comes out nearly uniform: no key matches, softmax spreads. That flatness is not a bug; it's what “nothing relevant here” looks like, and it's why attention entropy is a usable diagnostic for dead queries.

Quiz: In camera-LiDAR fusion where BEV queries attend to LiDAR, what exactly are Q, K, V — and what changes if you swap direction?

Q = projected BEV query vectors (n_q × d); K and V = projections of the LiDAR tokens (n_k × d). Output has the query side's shape: one fused vector per BEV cell. Swapping direction (LiDAR tokens as Q attending to BEV) would instead update the LiDAR tokens — the side you want enriched must own Q.

Quiz: Why divide by √d before softmax?

For d independent zero-mean unit-variance components, q·k has variance d, so raw logits grow like √d. Large logits push softmax into its saturated regime — near one-hot outputs with vanishing gradients everywhere else. Dividing by √d keeps logit variance ≈ 1 regardless of dimension.

Quiz: A BEV query's attention row is nearly uniform. Two interpretations?

Benign: nothing in the key set is relevant to that cell (empty space) — uniform is the honest answer. Pathological: the query has collapsed or the keys are undertrained, so every row is uniform. Distinguish by checking whether entropy varies across queries; uniform-everywhere means the head is dead.

Within mid fusion, the mechanism menu: token concatenation (throw all modality tokens into one self-attention pot — simplest, O((n₁+n₂)²) cost, no explicit geometry); cross-attention (Section 5 — directional, geometry-aware via query projection, the BEVFormer/TransFusion pattern); BEV-space concat/sum (rasterize every modality to the same BEV grid and concatenate channels, then convolve — the BEVFusion pattern; cheapest and remarkably strong because alignment is handled by geometry, not learned). Quiz: The interviewer asks "why not late fusion everywhere — it's the most robust?"

Late fusion can only combine what each detector already found: a camera-invisible (night) pedestrian that LiDAR half-sees at 3 points gets rejected by both single-modality detectors, and no box-level vote can resurrect it. Mid fusion lets weak evidence from two sensors reinforce below the detection threshold. Answer: mid fusion for performance, and a late-fused lightweight path retained as the independent safety fallback.

Quiz: Why is BEV-space concatenation such a strong baseline despite being "dumb"?

Because the hard part of fusion is spatial alignment, and rasterizing both modalities into the same metric BEV grid solves alignment with calibrated geometry instead of asking attention to learn it. What remains — mixing aligned channels — is easy for plain convolutions.

7. Training the encoder

Order your answer as: supervised heads (the baseline), then self-supervised pretraining (MAE for pixels, contrastive for cross-modal alignment), then distillation, then the multi-task balancing problem. That order is a maturity ladder — labels are expensive at AV scale, so everything after "supervised" is about squeezing the ocean of unlabeled fleet data.

7a. MAE: masked autoencoding

7b. Contrastive alignment (CLIP-style / InfoNCE)

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{InfoNCE}} = -\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B} \log \frac{\exp(\text{sim}(u_i, w_i)/\tau)}{\sum_{j=1}^{B} \exp(\text{sim}(u_i, w_j)/\tau)}$$

u = one modality's embedding, w = the other's, sim = cosine similarity, τ = temperature. Matched pairs (same scene region from camera and LiDAR, or image↔caption) are pulled together; the rest of the batch serves as negatives.

Distillation: run a huge teacher (or a LiDAR-privileged model) offboard, train the onboard encoder to match its features or logits — the standard way to buy accuracy under the latency budget, and the bridge to your quantization story. Multi-task balancing: with detection + occupancy + forecasting heads on one BEV trunk, naive loss summing lets one task's gradient scale dominate. Name the tools: per-task loss weights \( \mathcal{L}=\sum_i \lambda_i \mathcal{L}_i \), uncertainty weighting (learn λ as homoscedastic noise), gradient normalization (GradNorm), or simply decoupled head learning rates. And say the honest thing: in practice teams tune λ on the metric that matters and monitor per-task validation curves for one task regressing while the total loss improves.

Quiz: Why does MAE mask 75% of patches, when BERT masks 15% of words?

Images are spatially redundant — with 15% masked, reconstruction succeeds by local interpolation and the model learns nothing semantic. Masking 75% forces reconstruction from scene-level understanding. Bonus: the encoder only processes the 25% visible tokens, making pretraining ~3–4× cheaper per image.

Quiz: What does the temperature τ do in InfoNCE, and what happens at the extremes?

τ scales the logits before softmax, controlling how hard the loss focuses on hard negatives. τ→0: near-argmax — gradients concentrate entirely on the single hardest negative, training gets unstable. τ large: softmax flattens, all negatives count equally, gradients weaken. CLIP learns τ; it settles around 0.01–0.07.

Quiz: Detection mAP improves while occupancy IoU quietly regresses over a training run. What's happening and what do you check?

Negative transfer / task imbalance: the shared BEV trunk is being pulled toward detection-favorable features (total loss still falls, so it's invisible in aggregate). Check per-task gradient norms on the trunk and per-task validation curves; fix with loss re-weighting, GradNorm/uncertainty weighting, or partially decoupling the trunks.

8. Robustness and production reality

8a. Sensor dropout: train for the failure you will have

Calibration and extrinsics drift. Every geometric fusion op (BEV projection, query sampling, point painting) routes through the camera intrinsics K and sensor-to-vehicle extrinsics [R|t]. Mounts flex with temperature, bumps, and time — a 0.5° extrinsic yaw error displaces a projection by ~0.9 m at 100 m range, silently corrupting long-range fusion while nearby objects look fine. Production answers: online self-calibration (estimate drift from ego-motion + feature consistency), calibration-jitter augmentation at training time so the net tolerates small errors, and monitoring cross-modal residuals (do LiDAR edges land on image edges?) as a health signal.

Temporal fusion. Single frames can't disambiguate parked-vs-stopped or see through momentary occlusion. Standard pattern: keep a sliding window of past BEV features, warp each into the current ego frame using ego-motion (this compensation is the crucial detail — otherwise the world smears when you move), then fuse by concatenation or recurrent/temporal attention. This is also where the encoder starts overlapping with world models: predict-the-next-BEV is both a pretraining objective and a forecasting head.

Uncertainty heads. Detection confidence is not calibrated probability. Production encoders add explicit uncertainty: per-box variance heads (learned via Gaussian NLL — the loss \( \frac{\|y-\hat y\|^2}{2\sigma^2} + \frac{1}{2}\log\sigma^2 \) lets the net "pay" to admit ignorance), ensemble or MC-dropout disagreement for epistemic uncertainty, and post-hoc temperature scaling for classification calibration. Downstream planning treats a low-confidence pedestrian differently from a confident one — uncertainty is an output, not a debugging artifact.

Quiz: Why does sensor-dropout training work, mechanistically, in an attention-based fusion module?

Attention renormalizes over whatever keys are present. If training sometimes removes all LiDAR tokens, the model is forced to learn camera- and radar-conditional pathways that produce sane outputs; softmax then redistributes attention mass over remaining tokens at runtime. Without such training, the fused features at inference are out-of-distribution the moment a branch dies, and outputs are garbage rather than degraded.

Quiz: 0.5° of extrinsic yaw error — why does long-range fusion fail first?

Angular error converts to lateral displacement linearly with range: at 10 m it's ~9 cm (sub-cell, invisible); at 100 m it's ~0.9 m — several BEV cells, so camera features get fused into the wrong cells exactly where LiDAR is sparsest and camera evidence matters most. Detection quality degrades from the far field inward, which is also the diagnostic signature to look for.

Quiz: What breaks if you fuse past BEV frames without ego-motion compensation?

Static world structure smears along the ego trajectory — a parked car appears at a different grid cell each frame, so temporal aggregation blurs it instead of sharpening it, and the net learns to distrust history. Warp past features into the current ego frame first; then static things stack and moving things leave interpretable motion traces.

9. Where this encoder feeds VLMs, VLAs, and world models

The BEV-token encoder is not the end of the road; it's the perception front-end for three bigger systems, and closing with this shows you see the field's direction:

  • VLM copilots / foundation models: the same tokens that feed detection heads can be projected into a language model's embedding space (a learned adapter, exactly like a vision encoder feeding an LLM). Then the AV stack can answer "why did you brake?" and do open-vocabulary detection of classes never labeled — the long-tail escape hatch.
  • VLA-style end-to-end driving: instead of handing boxes to a planner, hand encoder tokens to a policy head that outputs trajectories — the same encoder-tokens-to-actions pattern as VLA policies on robot arms. The encoder's quality bounds the policy; interfaces (tokens, not boxes) preserve information the hand-designed box abstraction throws away.
  • World models: predict future BEV / occupancy / latent states from past ones. This gives you (a) a pretraining objective on unlabeled fleet logs, (b) a forecasting head, and (c) a neural simulator for closed-loop evaluation and synthetic data — which loops back to training long-tail scenarios you can't collect safely.

One closing sentence for the interview: “Design the encoder so its tokens are a good interface — detection heads today, language adapters and policy/world-model heads tomorrow, without retraining the trunk.”

Quiz: Why do end-to-end driving stacks pass tokens rather than detected boxes to the planner/policy?

Boxes are a lossy hand-designed bottleneck: they discard shape detail, uncertainty structure, appearance, and everything about the scene that isn't a discrete object. Tokens keep the information and let the policy learn which of it matters — the same reason VLA policies consume vision-encoder tokens rather than a detector's output. The cost is interpretability, which is why hybrid stacks keep supervised heads as auxiliary outputs.
