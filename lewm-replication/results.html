We got 84%. The paper says 96%.

If the paper were right we'd expect about 9.6 of every 10 episodes to succeed. We got about 8.4.

Honestly: across every episode we ran, we get 84%; the paper reports 96% — about 12 points short. The 81-point lift over random rules out "the model is broken," but the gap to the paper is real.

The numbers behind this

Success across all episodes is 970/1,150 = 84.35% from 23 runs × 50 episodes (95% range 82.13–86.33) — an 81-point lift over the 3.33% random baseline (5/150), and 11.65 points below the paper's 96.0%. A one-sided test of our 970/1,150 against the paper's claim puts the odds this is sampling noise at p ≈ 3×10⁻⁵⁴ — effectively zero. The paper reports no range for its 96.0%.

Every seed agrees — and so does the second task.

Maybe one unlucky run dragged us down? No — here are all 23 runs at once. Not one touches the paper's line.

And the picture holds on a second task we evaluated independently — preliminary, but consistent.

The numbers behind this

Best run 92.0%; per-run range 74.0–92.0%; std 5.18 across 23 runs of 50 episodes each. That spread almost exactly matches what you'd expect from drawing 50 episodes at an 84% success rate — so the variation is sampling, and the gap to the paper is in the average. TwoRoom: 134/150 = 89.33% across 3 runs × 50 episodes (95% range 83.38–93.33). See the TwoRoom page for the full second-task story.

Why? Three honest guesses — we rank none.

We genuinely don't know which caused the gap. So we won't pretend one is likelier — here are all three, equal weight.

The scorecard differs

The paper doesn't fully document its evaluation setup — success threshold, episode pool, step budget. One knob set differently could move success more than 10 points.

How we'd test it: re-run with the original evaluation code, holding our model fixed.

Released weights ≠ paper-final

The public checkpoint may be an earlier or ablation-stage model, not the one that produced the headline 96%. There is no released training log to check.

How we'd test it: compare the released weights against a checkpoint we re-train ourselves.

The code we run drifted

We run against an unreleased newer vendor copy of the library; subtle planner or numerics changes would propagate silently into success rate.

How we'd test it: re-run on the published library version, holding everything else fixed.

Honestly: none of these means "the model is broken" (the 81-point lift over random rules that out) or "the paper is wrong." Under documented config and the released weights, we get 84%. A controlled re-run — the tests above — is the only way to attribute the gap, and the hypotheses aren't mutually exclusive.

The subtler finding.

The quiet part: at the horizon the planner actually uses, the world model barely beats guessing "nothing changes."

8% how much the predictor beats "assume nothing changed," 5 steps ahead

Five steps ahead — the horizon the planner works at — the predictor's error is only about 8% below the trivial predict-last baseline. So most of the lift toward 84% comes from the planner searching, not from the predictor's raw accuracy — and a weaker-than-paper predictor would also be consistent with the second guess above.

The full error curve and methodology live on the World Model page. Every number on this site is derived from the checked-in artifacts — expand below for the paths.

The numbers and where they come from

At the planner's working horizon (k=5), predictor error is 0.953 versus the predict-last baseline's 1.038 — 8.2% lower. Underlying run data:

  • 23 Push-T runs (50 episodes each): outputs/faithful/pusht/seed{0..22}/result.json
  • 3 TwoRoom runs: outputs/faithful/tworoom/seed{0,1,2}/result.json
  • 3 random-policy Push-T runs: outputs/faithful/pusht_random/seed{0,1,2}/result.json
  • Aggregated master data: outputs/demo/data/site_data.json

Raw counts: pooled Push-T 970/1,150; pooled TwoRoom 134/150; pooled random Push-T 5/150. 95% ranges and the noise test are computed in tools/t2h_aggregate.py — no smoothing, no seed exclusion. The seed-to-seed std (5.18) matches the binomial expectation √(0.84·0.16/50) ≈ 0.052; the paper's reported 2.83 is about half that, which is itself reproducibility-relevant.

Plain-language glossary
latent
The compressed code for one picture: 192 numbers. The model only ever works with these, never with pixels.
encoder
The part that turns a picture into those 192 numbers.
predictor
The part that guesses the next 192 numbers, given the last few and a planned move.
JEPA
Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture — the design choice to predict in that 192-number code instead of redrawing pixels.
CEM-MPC (the planner)
How the model acts: imagine many short plans, score each, keep the best, refine, take one step, then look again and repeat.
horizon
How many steps ahead it looks at once (here, 5).
goal latent
You give it a picture of the finished task; it encodes that to 192 numbers and steers toward them.
predict-last baseline
The dumb yardstick: guess nothing changes. The model has to beat this to be useful.
NN-decode
Since the model can't draw its guess, we show the closest real training frame to it. It's a stand-in, not a picture the model made.
