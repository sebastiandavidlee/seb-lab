The Planner — CEM-MPC turns a world model into a controller

LeWorldModel has no policy network. To choose an action, the system samples 300 candidate 5-step action sequences, rolls each forward through the latent predictor, scores them by squared distance to a goal latent, and refits a Gaussian over the top-30 elites for 30 iterations. It then executes the first action and replans. This is the Cross-Entropy Method applied as Model-Predictive Control (CEM-MPC) — sampling-based, no gradients flow through the planner. The model is used as a cost oracle; the planner is the policy.

1. Why MPC, not a learned policy

LeWorldModel does not contain a policy network. The model just predicts latents: given a context latent and an action sequence, the predictor outputs the next latent. To act, we treat the model as a cost oracle and search action sequences against it. This is Model-Predictive Control (MPC) — the model is the policy, in the sense that every action choice is the argmin of an optimization over the model's predictions, not the output of a feed-forward network trained on demonstrations.

Trade-off: no behavior-cloning step, no policy distillation, no actor-critic. The cost of acting is paid online, every step, in forward passes through the predictor (see §6 below).

2. CEM in 60 seconds

The Cross-Entropy Method (CEM) is sampling-based optimization. No gradients. The planner never differentiates the cost — it only evaluates it. Each CEM iteration does five things:

  1. Sample 300 candidate action sequences from a Gaussian over the H=5 future actions.
  2. Roll each candidate forward 5 steps in latent space using the predictor (the world model from the previous page).
  3. Score each candidate by squared distance from the predicted final latent to the goal latent.
  4. Keep the top-30 elites (lowest cost).
  5. Refit the Gaussian's mean and per-dimension std to those elites; repeat for 30 iterations.

After the last iteration, the first action of the best sequence is executed in the environment. Everything else is discarded.

A useful metaphor: think evolutionary breeding. Spawn many candidate plans, keep the best, breed from them, repeat. CEM is not random search — the population narrows toward low-cost regions iteration by iteration.

3. The cost function lives in latent space

The cost CEM minimises is ‖ẑ_predicted − z_goal‖² — squared Euclidean distance in the 192-d latent space, not in pixels. This is load-bearing: there is no pixel decoder in LeWorldModel, so a pixel-space cost would be impossible to evaluate against the model's predictions.

The goal is supplied as an image of the desired Push-T configuration. The pipeline is:

goal image → encoder (ViT-tiny) → z_goal (192-d) → distance(ẑ_t, z_goal) at each predicted step.

z_goal is computed once at the start of the episode and reused every step. The encoder is the same one trained with the predictor (see the World Model page); the goal latent and the predicted latents therefore live in the same learned space, which is what makes their squared distance a meaningful cost.

What this rules out: any cost term that depends on un-encoded RGB. What it does not rule out: the cost being well-shaped only locally around z_goal — squared distance in a learned 192-d space is not guaranteed to be convex, smooth, or even monotone with task progress. CEM tolerates this because it never differentiates the cost; it only ranks samples.

4. CEM convergence trace — the distribution actually collapses

Two things to read off this plot. First, elite cost falls by two orders of magnitude — the Gaussian is finding low-cost regions, not just averaging over random samples. Second, action std falls by ~4× — the population is concentrating, not staying diffuse. Both curves are monotonic over the 30-iteration budget, so the budget is not obviously over-spent (no long flat tail) or under-spent (no still-falling cost at iteration 30 of the order of the early drop).

5. Receding horizon — closed-loop replanning

A useful metaphor: GPS rerouting. You don't compute the entire route once and then follow it blindly — the GPS recomputes constantly given where you actually are. MPC is the same. The planner produces a 5-step plan, executes only the first action, observes the new env state, and re-plans from scratch.

Why throw away 4 of the 5 planned actions? Because the predictor's accuracy decays with horizon (see the World Model page's latent-error-vs-horizon plot): predicted latents at k=5 are already meaningfully worse than at k=1, so committing to the later steps of the plan would mean acting on increasingly stale predictions. Replanning each step keeps every executed action grounded in a fresh observation.

6. Compute cost per step

One CEM call = 300 samples × 5-step rollout × 30 iterations = 45,000 forward passes through the latent predictor, per env step. The predictor is small (6-layer transformer over 192-d latents), so on the RTX 4080 a full Push-T episode (50 env steps, ≈30 s of CEM wall-time per step batched) takes about 60 s end-to-end. The encoder runs once per env step on the new observation.

Key knobs (and their values in this replication): horizon H=5, samples N=300, iterations T=30, elite fraction 0.1 (30 elites), action block = 5. Halving any of {N, T, H} roughly halves planning cost — and, empirically in the original paper, costs success rate. We did not sweep these knobs in this replication.

7. What this means for the world model

The planner does the heavy lifting. The world model only needs to be locally accurate — a few steps ahead — because CEM only plans H=5 steps deep and then the loop closes on a fresh observation. Far-horizon predictions matter less: they are never the basis of an executed action.

This is why the predictor's poor k≥10 accuracy (see the World Model page) does not, by itself, tank the system. The MPC loop is designed exactly around the assumption that long-horizon prediction is unreliable, and it limits the planner's exposure to that unreliability by replanning every env step. The honest version of this argument: the system works despite the predictor's mediocre k=5 MSE (only ~8% below a predict-last baseline), not because the predictor is itself a strong forward model.

The CEM trace shown above is a single Push-T episode. The convergence picture is consistent across logged episodes, but we have not plotted distribution-over-episodes of the per-iter trace; treat the 100×/4× numbers as a representative point estimate, not an aggregate.
