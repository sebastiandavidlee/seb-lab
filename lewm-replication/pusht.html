A round puck nudges a gray T into a green outline. That's the whole game.

The puck has no gripper and no suction — it can only move the T by bumping into it.

What the model sees, does, and is graded on

Three things define the task — what it sees, what it can do, and when it wins.

The fine print

Each episode gives the puck a budget of 50 steps to finish. Training frames come from the quentinll/lewm-pusht dataset (thousands of expert demonstrations). Success is scored as overlap measured by intersection-over-union between the T and the goal footprint.

Why it's hard: the T fights back

Push the T off-center and it spins — so the puck spends most of its time circling around to set up the next clean push.

Because the puck can't grab the block, it's like parallel parking a T with only a round bumper: you can't rotate the object directly, only plan around the spin you just caused.

When does it count as a win?

Cover enough of the green outline with the gray T, hold it there, and you win.

There's no shaped reward and no near-miss bonus. The episode crosses the line or it doesn't.

Plain-language glossary
latent
The 192 numbers the model uses instead of pixels.
encoder
The part that turns a picture into those 192 numbers.
predictor
The part that guesses the next 192 numbers, given the last few and a planned move.
JEPA
Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture — predicts in number-space, never redrawing the image.
CEM-MPC (the planner)
Imagine many short action plans, score each, keep the best, refine, take one step, then look again and repeat.
horizon
How many steps ahead it looks at once (here, 5).
goal latent
A picture of the finished task, encoded to 192 numbers, that the planner steers toward.
NN-decode
The model can't draw its guess, so we show the closest real training frame to it — a stand-in, not a picture the model made.
